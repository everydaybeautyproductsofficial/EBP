The Truth About Hair Loss on GLP-1 Drugs

GLP-1 hair loss is more common than people admit. Here's what the new tirzepatide vs. semaglutide research actually shows, why it happens, and how to treat it.

P
Priya Anand

— Min Read

If your hair has been coming out in the shower more than usual since you started a GLP-1 medication, you're not imagining it — and you're definitely not alone. GLP-1 hair loss has become one of the most talked-about side effects of drugs like Ozempic, Wegovy, Zepbound, and Mounjaro, and new research is finally putting some real numbers behind what people have been posting about online for months.

Here's the part that surprised even dermatologists: the risk isn't the same across every GLP-1 drug. Below, we break down what the newest study found, why it might be happening, and what actually helps if you're dealing with it right now.

GLP-1 and hair loss in females

Women make up the vast majority of people reporting hair thinning on these medications, and the data backs that up. Across recent research, women on tirzepatide developed hair loss at nearly double the rate of women on semaglutide — around 5.5% compared to 3.6% over a year of use. That's a meaningful gap, even though both numbers are technically "uncommon" in the bigger picture.

Part of this comes down to biology. Female pattern hair thinning is already more sensitive to hormone shifts, stress, and nutrient gaps than male pattern loss tends to be, so anything that changes your body quickly — including fast weight loss — tends to show up on the scalp first for women. Add in the fact that GLP-1 users skew female, and it's no surprise this has become such a hot topic in hair care circles lately.

Glp 1 hair loss zepbound

The newest study — not yet published in a medical journal — followed 12,863 people on semaglutide and 12,863 people on tirzepatide (the drug behind Zepbound and Mounjaro) for a full year. None of them had a documented history of alopecia going in. By the end of the study, 4.2% of tirzepatide users had developed hair loss, compared to 2.88% of semaglutide users.

Because tirzepatide tends to produce more weight loss overall, researchers also matched people by how much weight they'd actually lost. Even then, tirzepatide users still came out ahead on hair loss risk — 4.24% versus 3.33% for semaglutide, at the same amount of weight lost. The gap was visible as early as six months in and only widened from there. Venky Soundararajan, PhD, a co-author on the study, put it plainly: hair loss was uncommon overall, but real, and shouldn't be brushed aside.

It's worth remembering this was an observational study — it shows a link, not proof that tirzepatide directly causes hair loss. And separately, about half of women deal with some form of hair loss in their lifetime regardless of medication, so it's not always a clean cause-and-effect story.

GLP-1 hair loss DHT

So why does one drug seem worse than the other? The leading theory has to do with how each medication actually works in the body. Semaglutide targets a single receptor — GLP-1. Tirzepatide targets two: GLP-1 and a second one called GIP (glucose-dependent insulinotropic polypeptide). According to Joshua Zeichner, MD, director of Cosmetic and Clinical Research in Dermatology at Mount Sinai Hospital, GIP receptors have actually been found inside hair follicles themselves, which means tirzepatide may be interacting with the hair growth cycle in a way semaglutide doesn't.

There's also a hormonal thread here worth flagging: research has found that hair loss on GLP-1 medications tends to show up as one of two types — androgenetic alopecia, the hormone-and-DHT-driven pattern thinning most people picture when they hear "hair loss," or telogen effluvium, a more temporary, stress-triggered shedding. Semaglutide use has shown a slightly stronger link to the androgenetic (DHT-related) type, while tirzepatide has leaned more toward the telogen effluvium pattern. Neither link is fully proven, but it's a useful distinction because the two types are treated very differently.

Interestingly, women on tirzepatide who experienced hair loss were also more likely to have irregular periods, an underactive thyroid, or polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS) than women who didn't lose hair. That doesn't mean one causes the other, but it does suggest that existing hormonal sensitivity may make some women more prone to shedding on these drugs than others.

Is hair loss from GLP-1 permanent

Here's the reassuring part: in most cases, no. If your hair loss fits the telogen effluvium pattern — which is the more common type tied to rapid weight loss — your hair will typically grow back once your weight and body chemistry level out. You won't go bald, and you won't lose all your hair from this. It just takes patience, usually somewhere in the six-month-to-a-year range for a full recovery.

The picture is a little different for androgenetic alopecia, since that type is driven by genetics and hormones rather than a temporary shock to the system. It tends to respond well to treatment but rarely reverses fully on its own.

GLP-1 hair loss treatment

Dermatologists don't leave you empty-handed here. Dr. Zeichner points to a handful of treatments that consistently help, including minoxidil, finasteride, hair-specific vitamin supplements, red light therapy, and platelet-rich plasma (PRP) injections for more stubborn cases. Which one makes sense depends heavily on whether you're dealing with telogen effluvium or androgenetic alopecia, so a proper diagnosis matters before you spend money on anything.

How to stop hair loss from GLP-1 female

You can't control everything about how your body reacts to a GLP-1, but there's a lot within your control that genuinely moves the needle, especially for women. Here's where to start:

  1. Hit your protein target every day — most people on a GLP-1 are eating less overall, so protein is the first thing that quietly slips.
  2. Ask your doctor about slowing your weight loss pace if it's been unusually fast, since rapid loss is the biggest known trigger.
  3. Get your iron, vitamin D, zinc, and thyroid levels tested rather than guessing.
  4. Start a topical treatment early instead of waiting for shedding to get worse.
  5. Be gentle with your hair — skip tight hairstyles, harsh heat styling, and aggressive brushing while your hair is more fragile.
  6. See a board-certified dermatologist as soon as you notice more hair in your brush than usual, not months later.

This part matters more than people realize, because hair also changes texture and color when your body is under stress — something we talked through in more detail in our piece on how hair color and health are connected.

Our Final Verdict

GLP-1 hair loss is real, it's more common in women, and tirzepatide does appear to carry a higher risk than semaglutide based on the latest data. But "higher risk" doesn't mean guaranteed, and for most people, it's a temporary bump on the way to a bigger goal rather than a permanent change.

If you're already on a GLP-1 and noticing shedding, don't panic and don't quietly wait it out either. Get your levels checked, start a treatment that matches your actual type of hair loss, and give it time — most people do see their hair come back once their body settles into its new normal.

Frequently Asked Questions
Is There Really a $10 Hair Loss Cure? ▼
No single $10 product reverses hair loss, but a cheap, consistent basic — like an over-the-counter minoxidil solution or a daily biotin supplement — can genuinely help mild shedding. Be wary of viral "miracle cure" claims; real regrowth takes weeks of consistent use, not one bottle.
Is GLP-1 hair loss reversible? ▼
In most cases, yes. Because it's usually telogen effluvium tied to rapid weight loss, hair typically grows back once your weight and body chemistry stabilize. Full regrowth can take six months to a year.
How to keep from losing hair on a GLP one? ▼
Prioritize protein at every meal, keep your calorie deficit moderate rather than extreme, stay on top of iron and vitamin D levels, and loop in a dermatologist early if you notice shedding rather than waiting it out.
How to stop hair from falling out on Ozempic? ▼
The same weight-loss-related shedding seen with tirzepatide can happen on semaglutide too. Slowing the pace of weight loss, eating enough protein, and adding a scalp-focused treatment like minoxidil are the main levers that seem to help.
What are the best vitamins to help with GLP-1 hair loss? ▼
Iron, vitamin D, zinc, and biotin are the ones dermatologists check most often in patients with diet or medication-related shedding, since deficiencies in any of these can worsen hair loss on their own.
What are the top 3 vitamins for hair loss? ▼
Biotin, vitamin D, and iron tend to top most dermatologists' lists, since low levels of these three are the most frequently linked to noticeable shedding, especially in women.
What is the best supplement for GLP-1 users? ▼
There's no single "best" supplement for everyone, but a hair-specific multivitamin formulated for women — one that includes biotin, saw palmetto, and marine collagen — is a common starting point dermatologists suggest for weight-loss-related shedding.
What is a natural GLP-1 supplement? ▼
Some people use berberine, a plant compound sometimes nicknamed "nature's Ozempic," as a natural alternative that may support blood sugar and modest weight management, though it isn't regulated or proven to match prescription GLP-1 medications.
Why can't you eat eggs on Ozempic? ▼
You can eat eggs on Ozempic — there's no medical rule against it. The confusion likely comes from GLP-1 drugs slowing digestion, which can make heavier, richer foods like eggs feel more filling or slightly harder to digest for some users, not because eggs are unsafe.
Who cannot take GLP-1? ▼
People with a personal or family history of medullary thyroid cancer or Multiple Endocrine Neoplasia syndrome type 2, and those who are pregnant or breastfeeding, are generally advised against GLP-1 medications. Always confirm eligibility with a doctor first.
